8.1 · What Makes Up the Air We Breathe and Our Atmosphere?

Atmospheric Composition & Layers

Troposphere
Lowest 5–10 miles. Contains most atmospheric mass and all weather.
Stratosphere
Extends ~30 miles above troposphere. Contains the protective ozone layer.
Mesosphere
Coldest layer, temperatures to –148°F.
Thermosphere
Outermost layer — warm from solar/cosmic radiation, very low density.

8.2 · What Is Weather and How Does It Change?

Weather vs. Climate

  • Weather = short-term conditions (temperature, moisture, wind). Climate = 30-year average of regional weather patterns.
  • Temperature varies by latitude, land/water features, and elevation.
  • Severe drought — increasingly common with climate change — stresses trees, leaving them vulnerable to insects and disease.
  • Wind flows from high to low pressure. Lake-effect snow forms when cold winds cross warmer water.
  • Mountain ranges force air upward, cooling it and releasing precipitation on the windward side.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
8.2 · Water Vapor and Humidity

Water in the Air

Precipitation, humidity, and cloud cover all reflect water vapor in the air — which moves in and out of the atmosphere by evaporation and condensation.

  • Humidity is the amount of water in a given volume of air; it strongly affects evaporation rates.
  • Relative humidity is the water in the air as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature.
  • Plants add water to the atmosphere through transpiration.

8.4 · How Does the Atmosphere Become Polluted?

Where Does Pollution Come From?

From Chapter 3: matter is conserved. When we burn wood or fuel. The matter doesn't disappear — it changes form and persists as emissions in the atmosphere.

What Is Air Pollution?
Adding materials to the atmosphere that harm the health of humans or other organisms.
Examples
Burning fossil fuels leads to the release of significant amounts of carbon dioxide. This is the largest contributor to anthropogenic climate change.
Two Categories
Pollutants are either primary (harmful as emitted) or secondary (formed by reactions in the atmosphere).

8.4 · How Does the Atmosphere Become Polluted? — Primary Pollutants

Primary Pollutants

Carbon Monoxide (CO)
From incomplete combustion in idling vehicles. Displaces oxygen in blood.
Particulate Matter (PM)
Particles <10μm suspended in air. Smallest particles penetrate lungs and enter bloodstream. Linked to respiratory health problems.
Toxic Metals
Lead, mercury, arsenic travel in airborne particles. Lead is a neurotoxin causing developmental damage.
VOCs
Volatile organic compounds from fossil fuel burning and solvents. Higher concentrations indoors.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
8.4 · Secondary Pollutants

From Reaction to Pollution

Secondary Pollutants
Formed by atmospheric reactions (not directly emitted). Smog (VOCs and NOx from fossil fuels), ground-level ozone, and acid rain are all secondary pollutants.
Thermal Inversion
Warm air caps a cooler layer below, trapping pollutants near the surface. Prevents normal vertical mixing and creates sustained smog events.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
8.5 · What Is Happening to the Ozone Layer?

The Ozone Layer

  • Stratospheric ozone (ozone layer) blocks 99% of incoming UV radiation, protecting all terrestrial life.
  • 1970s: Lovelock, Rowland, and Molina discovered CFCs (refrigerants) accumulate in the stratosphere.
  • By the 1980s, satellite data confirmed thinning. An ozone hole over Antarctica showed 50% depletion.
  • Important: ground level ozone is an air pollutant and harmful to human beings.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
8.6 · What Is Acid Rain?

Acid Rain

  • Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrous oxide (NOₓ) from vehicles, agriculture, and industry react with water vapor to form sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) and nitric acid (HNO₃), which fall as acid rain, snow, or fog.
  • Normal rainwater pH: 5.6. Acid deposition (acid rain) brings pH well below that threshold.
  • Freshwater ecosystems naturally range pH 6–8. Lower pH disrupts insect and fish development.

8.6 · Consequences of Air Pollution

Air pollution rarely makes the news, yet it is one of the largest causes of death in the world. Each comparison sets a cause of death linked to air pollution beside one that gets far more attention. Estimate the deaths per year worldwide; then the figures are revealed beside the class’s estimates.

Sources

Deaths from air pollution are estimated, not counted. No death certificate says “air pollution”: epidemiologists combine measured exposure with long-term studies of how exposure raises the risk of heart disease, stroke, lung cancer, COPD, and pneumonia. Estimates vary between research groups and years. The categories overlap, too: tailpipe deaths are part of outdoor air pollution deaths, so the numbers here should not be added together. Deaths from crashes and homicide are counted from records, though many countries’ records are incomplete.

  1. World Health Organization. Household air pollution (fact sheet). 2.9 million deaths in 2021, including over 309,000 children under 5; about 2.1 billion people cook with open fires or inefficient stoves fueled by kerosene, biomass, or coal.
  2. World Health Organization (2024). Ambient (outdoor) air quality and health (fact sheet). 4.2 million premature deaths in 2019; 89% in low- and middle-income countries.
  3. World Health Organization. Air pollution (health topic). The combined effects of ambient and household air pollution are associated with 6.7 million premature deaths a year.
  4. Anenberg, S. C., Miller, J., Henze, D. K., Minjares, R., & Achakulwisut, P. (2019). The global burden of transportation tailpipe emissions on air pollution-related mortality in 2010 and 2015. Environmental Research Letters, 14(9), 094012. About 385,000 deaths in 2015 (11.4% of ambient PM₂.₅ and ozone deaths); about 181,000 from on-road diesel.
  5. World Health Organization (2023). Global Status Report on Road Safety 2023. 1.19 million road traffic deaths a year (2021); leading cause of death for ages 5–29.
  6. GBD 2019 Snakebite Envenomation Collaborators (2022). Global mortality of snakebite envenoming between 1990 and 2019. Nature Communications, 13, 6160. 63,400 deaths in 2019 (95% uncertainty interval 38,900–78,600).
  7. World Health Organization. Snakebite envenoming. An estimated 81,410–137,880 deaths a year; many bites go unreported.
  8. United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (2023). Global Study on Homicide 2023. 458,000 homicides in 2021, more than armed conflict and terrorism combined.
Air pollution is one of the greatest environmental risks to health: the WHO links outdoor and household air pollution together to about 6.7 million premature deaths a year. Those deaths rarely make headlines, because they come slowly, as heart attacks, strokes, and lung disease, and not as a single event.
8.7 · How Are We Responding to Pollution in the Atmosphere?

US Air Policy: Three Strategies

Clean Air Act (1970)
Established national air quality standards and regulations on emission sources. Has reduced major pollutants even as population grew.
CAFE Standards (1975)
Energy Policy and Conservation Act set minimum mpg standards for cars and light trucks, driving fuel efficiency improvements.
Cap-and-Trade (1990)
Amendments added tradeable emission allowances. EPA sets total allowable emissions; utilities buy, sell, trade, or bank allowances, a market-driven approach.

Method & sources

The same ledger method as the previous slide, applied to EPA’s own accounting of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments. Costs are direct compliance spending: what firms and governments pay for equipment, cleaner fuels, and operations, before any of it is passed on in prices. Values marked ≈ are computed from the shares EPA reports; all values are in 2006 dollars.

  1. U.S. EPA Office of Air and Radiation (2011). The Benefits and Costs of the Clean Air Act from 1990 to 2020 (Second Section 812 Prospective Study, final report).
